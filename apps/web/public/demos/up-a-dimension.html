Up a Dimension

Five 10-minute kitchen-table demos about space, scale and leverage, for ages 6–12. Do the real thing with your hands first, then play with the screen.

🐜 The water ring

You need
  • A plate or tray, a small upside-down cup (the island), water
  • A tiny toy figure and a drinking straw
Do it
  1. Put the cup upside-down in the middle of the plate and pour water around it.
  2. Stand the figure on the cup. It lives on a flat world and can't swim. How does it get to the dry rim?
  3. Let them try. Then lay the straw from the cup to the rim, from above.
Tap the 🏁 to make the ant walk there.
Ask: Why couldn't the ant get out? Where did the help come from?
"From up here it is easy; from inside it was impossible."
The ant only knows forward, back, left and right. The straw comes from a third direction, up, which it can't even point to. When you are stuck, add a dimension: in geometry that is drawing a line that wasn't in the problem.

🔺 Six matchsticks

You need
  • Six sticks of the same length: toothpicks, straws or unlit matches
  • A little play-dough or tape for the corners
Do it
  1. The puzzle: make four triangles, every side exactly one stick, using only six sticks.
  2. Let them try flat on the table for two minutes. (It can't be done.)
  3. Hint, if needed: "Who said it has to stay on the table?"
Drag the picture to turn it.
Ask: Why was it impossible flat? What changed?
"Go up a dimension."
Flat, the three sticks that run to the middle are too short to match the outside ones (0.58 of a stick). Lift the middle to 0.82 of a stick high and all six match: a tetrahedron, four equal triangles. Nothing was added except a direction.
Big idea · the space can change

📄 Folded paper

You need
  • A strip of paper about 20 cm long, a pen
Do it
  1. Mark A at one end and B at the other. An ant walking on the paper from A to B goes 20 cm.
  2. Challenge: make A touch B without cutting or tearing.
  3. They will bend or fold it. Measure the paper again: still 20 cm.
Ask: Did the paper road get shorter? Then how did A reach B?
"The road didn't get shorter; the space changed."
The path along the paper stays 20 cm. Bending uses the third direction, so A and B meet through the air. This is the picture behind "wormholes" in science fiction; in real physics they are only math so far, and nobody has made one. Being honest about that is part of the lesson.

🔢 Twenty doublings

You need
  • A calculator, or the buttons below
Do it
  1. First, everyone guesses: how many times do you double 1 to pass a million? Write the guesses down.
  2. Start at 1 and press ×2 again and again, counting the taps.
  3. Compare with stepping +1 at a time.

Ask: Which gets to a million faster, stepping or doubling? Why?
"Leap by doubling, not by stepping."
Twenty doublings give 1,048,576. Counting one per second, a million takes about 11½ days; doubling took 20 taps. Computers use the same trick backwards: you can find one name among a million sorted names in 20 yes/no questions (binary search).
Big idea · small at the core, big at the tip

📏 The ruler lever

You need
  • A 30 cm ruler and a round pencil
Do it
  1. Lay the pencil on the table. Rest the ruler across it, with the pencil 3 cm from your end.
  2. Press your end down about 1 cm and watch the far end jump.
  3. Now try to hold the far end perfectly still just above a line on paper. Watch it shake.
Ask: Why did the far end move so much more? What happened to the shake?
"Small at the core, big at the tip — and the wobble gets big too."
The far end moves (long side ÷ short side) times as much as your hand: 27 ÷ 3 = 9 times. That is why a throw or a punch starts at the hips and ends at the hand. But the shake grows by the same 9 times, which is why careful work (writing, threading a needle) uses the fingers, not the shoulder.
